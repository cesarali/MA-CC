\section{Relational-reasoning prompt}
\label{app:relational-prompt}

The population is driven by one versioned prompt family,
\texttt{relational\_public\_ballot} version~1. Each call is compiled into two
chat messages. The system message establishes the agent's role, privacy
boundary, task, call-specific option mapping, and currently known facts. The
user message supplies the current vote and visible social source, then states
the response contract. The model therefore receives only the state available
to the focal agent at that microscopic update; it is never shown the global
vote vector, other agents' private facts, the ground-truth answer, or the
controller's sensor sample.

The display below reproduces one complete prompt from the audit of the minimal
Study~04 inspection episode ($N=24$, $q_c=12$, $b=12$). It is the first focal
update of population round~1 for Agent~17. Typography is annotation only:
\relvariable{orange} marks values instantiated anew for the call,
\relprivate{green} marks the focal agent's private state, and
\relsocial{blue} marks the visible social input. None of these colours or the
``SYSTEM MESSAGE''/``USER MESSAGE'' labels are sent to the model.

\begin{relpromptbox}{Audited focal-update prompt}
\small\raggedright
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\textbf{SYSTEM MESSAGE}

You are \relvariable{Agent 17}, one participant in a group reasoning problem.

Your goal is to identify the correct answer.

Participant identities persist across interactions.

Different participants know different facts about this problem. No single
participant is guaranteed to know everything needed to answer, and some
participants may have objectives that differ from yours. You do not know
which participants, if any, have different objectives.

Make your own decision, using:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item the facts you currently know;
  \item your current position;
  \item the public positions of the participants shown below.
\end{itemize}

You see only the participants shown in this interaction, and of each one you
see their vote and the fact they chose to share, if any.

Other participants will see the same of you: your vote and any fact you choose
to share, and nothing else. Your reason is your own record: it is not shown to
anyone.

\textbf{QUESTION}

\relvariable{Where is Davi relative to Hira?}

The available answers are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \relvariable{A) WEST}
  \item \relvariable{B) EAST}
  \item \relvariable{C) SOUTHWEST}
\end{itemize}

Exactly one of these answers is correct. Vote by its letter.

\textbf{YOUR CURRENT KNOWLEDGE}

\relprivate{You do not currently know any facts about this problem. You will
have to rely on what other participants tell you.}

\medskip
\textbf{USER MESSAGE}

\textbf{YOUR CURRENT POSITION}

\relprivate{Vote: SOUTHWEST}

\textbf{CURRENT SOCIAL INFORMATION}

\relsocial{Agent 25\\
Vote: B}

\textbf{DECISION}

Work out which option the facts available to you support, and vote for it.

Your reason should briefly explain your choice, for your own record.

Sharing a fact is the only way to pass information to other participants. You
may share exactly one of the facts you currently know by giving its identifier
in \texttt{shared\_fact\_id}, so that the participants who see your position
can use it too. Use ``none'' if you prefer to share nothing.

You may share only a fact listed under YOUR CURRENT KNOWLEDGE, by its exact
identifier. Do not invent facts, identifiers, or relationships that were not
given to you.

Keep your reason to at most three sentences.

Return only valid JSON:

\texttt{\{}\\
\quad\texttt{"vote": "<A | B | C>",}\\
\quad\texttt{"reason": "<brief private reason>",}\\
\quad\texttt{"shared\_fact\_id": "<none>"}\\
\texttt{\}}
\end{relpromptbox}

\paragraph{What changes between calls.}
The identity, query, available semantic answers, private facts, current vote,
visible sources, and admissible fact identifiers are bound from the live game
state. The semantic answers are independently permuted onto A/B/C for every
call. Thus the \relsocial{social vote B} above means \emph{EAST} only in this
instance; the persistent population state stores EAST, not the letter B. This
prevents a stable display position from becoming an unintended convention.

\paragraph{Ordinary and controlled social inputs.}
An uncontrolled update shows the vote and, when supplied, one valid fact from
an ordinary peer. In the audited example the visible source is the controller:
the controller's semantic target EAST is rendered as \relsocial{Vote: B} under
this call's permutation. The prompt deliberately does not identify that source
as a controller or authority. Under the \texttt{recommendation\_only} mode it
also supplies no fact, so the intervention can change the public vote state but
cannot directly enlarge the focal agent's knowledge set.

\paragraph{Initialization variant.}
Before social interaction, each of the 24 agents receives the same role, task,
privacy, fact-sharing, and JSON-contract blocks, but no current-position or
social-information block. Instead, the prompt states that no other participant
has yet declared a position and instructs the agent to decide from its local
facts alone. The resulting 24 ballots define the initial population state.

\paragraph{Response and information boundary.}
The accepted response contains a display-letter vote, a private reason of at
most three sentences, and either one fact identifier already in the agent's
knowledge set or \texttt{none}. The display letter is immediately mapped back
to its semantic relation. The reason is retained for audit but never shown to
another agent. Only the semantic vote and the explicitly selected fact may
enter a later prompt, which makes the public decision channel and the epistemic
transmission channel separately observable.

\paragraph{Audit check.}
The inspection episode recorded 72 distinct compiled prompt instances: 24
local-initialization prompts and 48 focal-update prompts (24 in each of two
population rounds). All 72 responses passed validation without a retry or
provider error. The prompt definition hash was constant across the episode,
whereas every instance hash was unique, confirming that one fixed template was
bound to 72 call-specific views.

\subsection{Epistemic conditions: receiver disposition and controller evidence}
\label{app:relational-epistemic-conditions}

The population game and the controller each expose one binary epistemic knob,
and the two are set independently. The first,
\texttt{receiver\_epistemic\_disposition}, decides what every agent is told
about the social channel it listens to. The second,
\texttt{controller\_evidence\_strategy}, decides which single fact the
controller is allowed to cite when it speaks. They live at
\texttt{game.options} and \texttt{control.options} respectively. Crossing them
gives the four conditions used throughout Study~08, recorded in each round
record as \texttt{derived\_epistemic\_condition}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Condition & Receiver disposition & Controller evidence \\
\midrule
\texttt{naive\_neutral}      & \texttt{naive}    & \texttt{neutral} \\
\texttt{naive\_strategic}    & \texttt{naive}    & \texttt{strategic} \\
\texttt{vigilant\_neutral}   & \texttt{vigilant} & \texttt{neutral} \\
\texttt{vigilant\_strategic} & \texttt{vigilant} & \texttt{strategic} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two axes act at different places in the loop. The receiver axis is a
\emph{text} intervention: it rewrites one fixed system block, identically for
every agent and every call of an episode. The controller axis is a
\emph{content} intervention: it changes no template text at all, only which
proposition of the frozen task the controller puts into its social slot. An
agent therefore cannot read the controller's strategy off the wording of its
prompt, and the two manipulations remain separately attributable.

\paragraph{Receiver epistemic disposition.}
This axis substitutes the \texttt{social\_environment} block, the second system
block of the manifest. Both variants are bound as \texttt{fixed}: they are part
of the episode's prompt definition rather than of any per-call view, so within
one cell every one of the episode's prompts carries the same wording.
\texttt{naive} states only that identities persist, and says nothing about
whether a visible participant is worth believing. \texttt{vigilant} adds an
explicit instruction to discount a bare recommendation and to check the
evidence actually in hand. The two blocks are reproduced below exactly as sent.

\begin{relpromptbox}{\texttt{social\_environment} --- \texttt{naive}}
\small\raggedright
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
Participant identities persist across interactions.
\end{relpromptbox}

\begin{relpromptbox}{\texttt{social\_environment} --- \texttt{vigilant}}
\small\raggedright
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
Participant identities persist across interactions.

\medskip
Some participants may have objectives that differ from yours. Do not accept a
recommendation merely because another participant gives it. Evaluate whether
the explicit evidence you actually have supports the conclusion, and remain
aware that a source may present only part of the relevant information.
\end{relpromptbox}

Nothing else in the prompt moves with this axis. In the audited grid the block
is estimated at 6 tokens under \texttt{naive} and 55 under \texttt{vigilant},
carrying the whole per-prompt difference of 160 versus 209 estimated tokens.
The block manifest --- names, order, roles, and bindings --- is byte-identical
across all four cells, because it records prompt \emph{structure}; the
condition is pinned by the \texttt{receiver\_epistemic\_disposition} field
of each cell's resolved configuration and is visible in the rendered text
itself.

The audited Study~04 prompt displayed earlier in this appendix predates this
factorization. It shows the older combined block, which asserts both
distributed information and possible objective divergence in one paragraph.
The factorized axis separates those two claims, and Study~08 uses only the
\texttt{naive}/\texttt{vigilant} pair above.

\paragraph{Controller evidence strategy.}
Evidence reaches the population only in the controller's message mode
\texttt{recommendation\_\hspace{0pt}plus\_\hspace{0pt}fact}, in which the
controller occupies its social slot with both a vote and exactly one fact of
the frozen task.
That fact is chosen once, before the episode runs, by a deterministic rule that
never consults a model:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \texttt{neutral} takes the first fact in the task's frozen fact order.
    The choice ignores which relation the controller advocates, so the cited
    evidence is fixed by the task alone.
  \item \texttt{strategic} takes the admissible fact whose relation points most
    nearly in the advocated direction. Each of the eight compass relations has
    a symbolic displacement vector; the controller scores every fact by the
    cosine between its relation vector and the advocated relation's vector,
    discards every fact scoring zero or below, and cites the survivor with the
    highest score, breaking ties toward the earlier fact. If no fact has
    positive alignment the configuration is rejected at launch rather than
    silently advocating without evidence.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{strategic} is thus the selective-evidence arm: the controller still
never fabricates anything, since only real facts of the task are admissible,
but it is free to cite the true statement that best flatters its target and to
stay silent about the rest. \texttt{neutral} removes that freedom while holding
the recommendation, the budget, and the number of exposures fixed, so the
difference between the arms isolates the value of evidence \emph{selection}.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
Study~08's task \texttt{task\_0001} asks where Zora is relative to Feni, with
frozen fact order $(\texttt{f1},\texttt{f2},\texttt{f3})$:
\texttt{f1}~``Zora is south of Maro'' (SOUTH), \texttt{f2}~``Maro is west of
Feni'' (WEST), and the distractor \texttt{f3}~``Garo is north of Belo''
(NORTH), which mentions neither query entity. The correct relation is
SOUTHWEST. The rules then resolve as follows.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Advocated relation & \texttt{neutral} & \texttt{strategic} \\
\midrule
SOUTHWEST (correct) & \texttt{f1} (SOUTH) & \texttt{f1} (SOUTH) \\
NORTHWEST           & \texttt{f1} (SOUTH) & \texttt{f2} (WEST) \\
NORTH               & \texttt{f1} (SOUTH) & \texttt{f3} (NORTH) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row is the informative one. Advocating NORTH, the neutral controller
must cite \texttt{f1}, whose southward relation openly contradicts its own
recommendation. The strategic controller instead cites \texttt{f3}: a true
statement, pointing north, about two entities irrelevant to the question. Under
\texttt{naive} the agent is given no reason to notice the substitution; under
\texttt{vigilant} it is told in so many words to check whether the evidence it
holds supports the conclusion.

\paragraph{Audited grid.}
The four conditions were run once end to end against a live model. Each cell is
one episode of $N=6$ agents over 4 population rounds with a single visible
social slot ($K=1$), sensor sample $q_c=3$, exact budget $b=3$, target
\texttt{correct}, \texttt{recommendation\_plus\_fact}, on
\texttt{gwdg/openai-gpt-oss-120b} at temperature~0 with seed \texttt{20260828}.
Each cell issued 30 calls --- 6 local-initialization ballots and $6\times4=24$
focal updates --- for 120 in total, matching the recorded request count
exactly, so no response required a retry.

In this particular task the two evidence arms coincide: the advocated relation
is the correct one, SOUTHWEST, and the tie between \texttt{f1} and \texttt{f2}
--- both at cosine $1/\sqrt{2}$ --- resolves to the earlier fact, which is also
the neutral rule's choice. All four cells therefore exposed \texttt{f1}, and the
evidence axis is degenerate here by construction. The grid is a prompt- and
plumbing-level audit of the four conditions, not an estimate of their effect;
the axis separates only on tasks and targets where alignment discriminates, as
the two lower rows of the worked example show.
