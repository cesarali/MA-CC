\section{Resource-Constrained Control Design}
\label{app:resource_constrained_control}

The theory in Section~5 separates three questions that are often conflated in controller design:
how strongly the population can be moved, how coherently controller influence is converted into that
motion, and how efficiently sensing and actuation resources are converted into useful current.
This appendix turns those quantities into a simple design prescription without introducing an
additional notion of efficiency.

\subsection{Minimum-cost control at prescribed response}
\label{app:min_cost_control}

Let
\[
\pi=(q_c,b,\theta,\beta,h_c,\ldots)
\]
denote a controller configuration, and let \(\rho(n)\) be the population-state occupancy over the
evaluation window. Since the state-local susceptibility is defined conditionally on advocacy,
the expected controller-induced response per round is
\begin{equation}
R(\pi)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{N}
\rho(n)\,
a_n(\pi)\,
\chi_{h_c}\!\left(\frac{n}{N}\right).
\label{eq:app_expected_response}
\end{equation}
A direct operational interaction cost is
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{op}}(\pi)
=
q_c
+
b\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho(n)a_n(\pi),
\label{eq:app_operational_cost}
\end{equation}
where the first term counts the agents sensed every round and the second counts the expected number
of controlled microscopic opportunities.

The most direct controller-design problem is therefore not to maximize an efficiency in isolation,
but to achieve a prescribed behavioral response at minimum cost,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\pi^\star
=
\arg\min_{\pi} C_{\mathrm{op}}(\pi)
\qquad
\text{subject to}
\qquad
R(\pi)\ge R_{\mathrm{req}} .
}
\label{eq:app_min_cost_problem}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for a fixed budget one may maximize the achievable response,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\pi^\star
=
\arg\max_{\pi} R(\pi)
\qquad
\text{subject to}
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{op}}(\pi)\le C_{\max}.
}
\label{eq:app_max_response_problem}
\end{equation}
These formulations make the practical question explicit: once the required response has been fixed,
how much sensing and actuation can be removed before performance falls below the desired level?

For the exact finite-bias \(q=1\) reference,
\begin{equation}
\chi_{h_c}(x)
=
[\sigma(h_c)-x]\Lambda_b,
\qquad
\Lambda_b
=
1-\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)^b .
\label{eq:app_susceptibility_recall}
\end{equation}
The marginal gain from one additional controlled opportunity is therefore
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{b+1}-\Lambda_b
=
\frac{1}{N}
\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)^b ,
\label{eq:app_marginal_actuation_gain}
\end{equation}
which decreases monotonically with \(b\). Thus stronger actuation continues to increase the response,
but each additional controlled opportunity contributes less than the previous one. Operationally,
\(b\) should therefore be increased only until the required response is reached, unless additional
response is itself worth the extra interaction cost.

The same logic applies to sensing. Increasing \(q_c\) can improve the controller's estimate of the
population state and thereby modify \(a_n\), but sensing is useful only insofar as this change improves
the required response. If a larger \(q_c\) increases acquired information while leaving \(R(\pi)\)
essentially unchanged, the additional sensing is redundant for the stated control objective.

\subsection{How the two efficiencies guide the feasible design}
\label{app:efficiency_guided_design}

The constrained problem above specifies \emph{how to pay less}. The two bounded efficiencies from
Section~5 specify whether a feasible controller is using its influence and its resources well.

The information--response efficiency is
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}(n)
=
\frac{
2a_n(1-a_n)\,
\chi_{h_c}(n/N)^2
}{
(\ln 2)\,T_{h_c}(n)
}
\le 1,
\label{eq:app_eta_ir}
\end{equation}
with
\[
T_{h_c}(n)=I(U;n'\mid n).
\]
For fixed state \(n\), \(T_{h_c}(n)\) measures the complete action-dependent change in the next-state
distribution, whereas \(\chi_{h_c}(n/N)\) retains only its coherent shift in mean target fraction.
Consequently, a reduction in \(\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}(n)\) indicates that additional controller influence is
being expressed increasingly through distributional changes that do not contribute proportionally to
the desired mean motion.

This gives a first design criterion: among controller settings that satisfy the required response and
have comparable operational cost, prefer regimes in which \(\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}\) remains high over the
states that are actually visited. Importantly, \(\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}\) is a diagnostic rather than a
stand-alone objective: a controller with negligible response can be uninteresting even if its
information--response conversion is nearly ideal.

The thermodynamic efficiency provides the complementary resource-conversion criterion,
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{th}}
=
\frac{
h_eJ_c
}{
h_cJ_c+I(n;Y)+\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}
}
\le 1 .
\label{eq:app_eta_th}
\end{equation}
Here \(h_cJ_c\) is generalized controller input work, \(I(n;Y)\) is the acquired sensing information,
and \(h_eJ_c\) is useful output against the opposing load. This relation makes two practical design
rules explicit.

First, more sensing is not automatically better. If increasing \(q_c\) raises \(I(n;Y)\) without a
proportional increase in useful current, the additional information increases the required resource
accounting without delivering corresponding output. Second, stronger microscopic bias is not
automatically better. Increasing \(h_c\) can increase \(J_c\), but it also raises the controller input
\(h_cJ_c\); therefore the weakest bias compatible with the required response or current is generally
the natural candidate in a minimum-cost design.

Taken together, the design prescription is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(i) specify the minimum acceptable response }R_{\mathrm{req}},\\
&\text{(ii) reduce }q_c\text{ until further reduction degrades that response},\\
&\text{(iii) reduce }b\text{ until further reduction degrades that response},\\
&\text{(iv) use the weakest }h_c\text{ compatible with the required response or current},\\
&\text{(v) among feasible controllers, prefer regimes with high }
\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}
\text{ and }
\eta_{\mathrm{th}} .
\end{aligned}
}
\label{eq:app_design_recipe}
\end{equation}
Thus susceptibility determines \emph{where control has leverage}, the cost constraint determines
\emph{how much control must be purchased}, \(\eta_{\mathrm{IR}}\) determines whether the resulting
influence is expressed coherently, and \(\eta_{\mathrm{th}}\) determines whether sensing and actuation
resources are converted efficiently into useful directed current.

\subsection{Finite-time and nonstationary interpretation}
\label{app:nonstationary_design}

No stationarity assumption is required in the construction above. Let
\begin{equation}
\rho_k(n)=\Pr(N_k=n)
\end{equation}
denote the transient population distribution at round \(k\). Over a finite horizon of \(H\) rounds,
the occupancy entering Eqs.~\eqref{eq:app_expected_response}--\eqref{eq:app_operational_cost} can be
interpreted as the finite-time visitation distribution
\begin{equation}
\bar{\rho}_H(n)
=
\frac{1}{H}
\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\rho_k(n).
\label{eq:app_finite_time_occupancy}
\end{equation}
Hence the operational design problem averages over the transient states actually visited during the
experiment rather than over an equilibrium distribution. Likewise, the thermodynamic efficiency
already retains the finite-time boundary contribution \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}\), which accounts for
the fact that the system distribution changes during the feedback cycle. A stationary distribution is
therefore only a special long-time limit of the present formulation, not an assumption required by it.
