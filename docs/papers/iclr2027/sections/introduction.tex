\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed not only as individual problem solvers, but as components of \emph{multi-agent systems} in which many autonomous agents communicate, exchange information, and collectively determine an outcome.
Recent work has shown that populations of LLM agents can exhibit genuinely collective phenomena, including spontaneous convention formation, amplification of population-level biases, and higher-order coordination that cannot be understood from isolated agents alone \citep{ashery2025emergent,riedl2026emergent}.
These observations raise a complementary question that becomes increasingly important as such systems are deployed:
\emph{how can an external agent control a collective of interacting LLMs?}
In particular, we are interested not in unconstrained steering---where a controller can inspect and modify the entire system---but in the more realistic regime in which the controller itself has limited resources.

We study this problem through a \emph{bounded feedback controller}.
At each population round, the controller observes only a finite random sample of agents and must use this partial observation to decide whether to intervene.
Its sensing capacity is therefore limited: the controller never observes the complete population state and its estimate is intrinsically noisy.
Its actuation capacity is limited as well: even when it decides to act, it may replace the social input in only a bounded number of interactions.
The resulting closed loop has the form
\begin{equation}
    N_t \longrightarrow Y_t \longrightarrow U_t \longrightarrow N_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $N_t$ denotes the population state, $Y_t$ the controller's partial observation, and $U_t$ its intervention.
This formulation makes imperfect information a consequence of finite sensing resources rather than an artificial source of observation noise, and separates two distinct constraints on control: \emph{how much of the system can be observed} and \emph{how much of it can be acted upon}.
Our central question is therefore:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How efficiently can a resource-constrained feedback controller steer an interacting population of LLM agents when both information and actuation are limited?}
\end{quote}

Controlling an LLM population, however, should involve more than controlling a population of stochastic finite-state machines.
The distinctive feature of language-model agents is precisely that their interactions can carry \emph{semantic information}: agents can possess different facts, communicate those facts through language, accumulate evidence, and reason over combinations of information.
We therefore introduce an explicit \emph{epistemic load} into the collective dynamics using relational reasoning tasks with distributed evidence.
The information required to solve each task is spread across the population so that the collective contains a complete proof while individual agents initially do not.
Each agent consequently carries two coupled states: a social state, represented by its current answer or vote $X_i(t)$, and an epistemic state, represented by the set of supporting facts $K_i(t)$ available to it.
Local language interactions can change either or both.
An agent may adopt another agent's answer through social influence without acquiring the evidence that supports it, while an explicitly communicated fact may expand its knowledge and subsequently alter its reasoning.
This distinction allows us to study how \emph{social influence, information propagation, and reasoning jointly determine controllability}.

This separation is particularly important for feedback control.
In our main setting, the controller communicates only a recommendation and does not provide task evidence.
It can therefore directly perturb the social dynamics but cannot directly increase the epistemic content of the population.
We can consequently distinguish whether an intervention changes what agents \emph{believe} from whether it changes what they \emph{know}.
Moreover, by allowing the controller target to disagree with the objectively correct answer, controllability and task performance become distinct quantities: a controller can successfully steer the population while simultaneously moving it away from truth.
This provides a controlled setting for asking when accumulated evidence makes a population resistant to external influence, when social pressure overwhelms reasoning, and whether the epistemic state of a collective changes the amount of control required to redirect it.

To quantify these processes, we treat sensing and actuation as two distinct
information channels.  Writing \(n_k\) for the current number of agents that
support the controller target, \(Y_k\) for the controller's noisy observation
of that count, \(U_k\) for the action selected from that observation, and
\(n_{k+1}\) for the target-support count after actuation, the two quantities are
\begin{align}
    I(n_k;Y_k)
    &\qquad \text{(sensing information)}, \\
    T_\pi(n)
    \equiv I(U_k;n_{k+1}\mid n_k=n)
    &\qquad \text{(action-to-population information)}.
\end{align}
The first measures information acquired by the sensor about the current
population state.  The second measures, at a fixed current state, how much the
controller action is expressed in the population state after the actuation
step; the subscript \(\pi\) indicates that this channel is induced by the chosen
feedback policy.  We define \(\pi\) and the complete control loop below.
These quantities are complemented by signed response statistics, population currents, and their fluctuations.
This perspective connects naturally to feedback thermodynamics, where measurement and control are treated as coupled information-processing operations \citep{horowitz2014second}, and to recent stochastic-thermodynamic formulations of social imitation, where probability currents, dynamical activity, entropy production, and uncertainty relations characterize collective dynamics without requiring a literal energetic interpretation \citep{irisarri2026stochastic}.
Importantly, thermodynamics is not the motivation for introducing the controller.
Rather, it provides a principled language for characterizing the \emph{resource--response trade-offs} generated by the bounded feedback problem: how much information is acquired, how much intervention is applied, how large and reliable a population response is produced, and where additional control resources cease to be useful.

Finally, we introduce a matched finite-population $q$-voter process as a classical reference model.
The reference system is subjected to the same population size, local interaction structure, finite sensing process, stochastic feedback policy, and bounded actuation schedule as the LLM population.
It therefore captures the response expected from a system whose collective dynamics arise from stochastic local imitation alone.
This comparison is deliberately not intended to claim that LLM agents are $q$-voters.
Instead, the $q$-voter provides a controlled null model in which semantic communication, distributed evidence, and language-based reasoning are absent.
Agreement with this reference identifies behavior that can already be explained by ordinary stochastic social influence; systematic departures reveal where the epistemic and reasoning capabilities of language-model agents modify the control dynamics.

Taken together, our goal is to develop a quantitative account of \emph{control efficiency in reasoning multi-agent systems}: how limited sensing and limited actuation are converted into directed changes of a collective state, and how this conversion is altered when the interacting agents communicate meaningful information and reason with it.
Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate control of an LLM collective as a \emph{resource-constrained feedback problem}, explicitly separating finite sensing capacity from finite actuation capacity and treating imperfect state information as a consequence of bounded observation.

    \item We introduce a relational multi-agent environment with distributed evidence that exposes both \emph{social} and \emph{epistemic} population dynamics, allowing us to distinguish changes in expressed belief from changes in the information available for reasoning.

    \item We develop information-theoretic and stochastic-dynamical observables that quantify sensing, transmission of controller actions, directional response, currents, fluctuations, and the efficiency with which control resources are converted into collective change.

    \item We construct a matched finite-$N$ $q$-voter reference under the same feedback protocol, providing a classical imitation baseline against which the specifically language- and reasoning-dependent behavior of the LLM population can be identified.
\end{itemize}
