\begin{center}
{\Large\textbf{What changes when the rounds go: Simulation 2 against Simulation 1}}\\[3pt]
{\small LLM-free simulator, task\_003, runs of 7 and 9 October 2026}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{The two simulations.} 24 exact-Bayesian agents and one controller posting
true facts on a shared board. \emph{Simulation 1}: days and nights; every agent acts once a
day; memories fade at dawn (each fact survives with probability $\rho$); the board is cleared
each night; the controller posts $b$ facts at once each night. \emph{Simulation 2}: no rounds;
agents and controller act at random times (controller rate $\lambda_c$); each fact has a random
lifetime (on average a fraction $\rho$ survives one time unit); readers see only the 48 most
recent posts; one fact per controller message, at most 30. \emph{Gain}: the controller's effect
on the agents' mean belief in its target at $t = 30$, against the same episode without a
controller. Compared below: Simulation 1 with $b = 1$, Simulation 2 with $\lambda_c = 1$; posts
read $q = 6$ (agents), $q_c = 12$ (controller); vote gate and proof stop on.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Asynchrony matters only through forgetting.} With full memory the two
  simulations agree within 0.01. When agents forget, Simulation 2 gives weaker control and a
  weaker consensus of the swarm on its own proof (bold entries; confirmed with fresh random
  draws). A reading, not yet tested: the rounds let agents hold many facts at once (dawn
  forgetting, then a full day of posts); with random timing, facts expire between actions.
  \emph{Simulation 1 overstated both the swarm's consensus and the controller's power when
  agents forget.}
\item \textbf{The backfire comes back by a different route.} In Simulation 1 more facts per
  night lowered the gain (the false controller's gain turned negative at $b = 9$) because each
  all-or-nothing post had to include facts against the target. Simulation 2 posts one fact at a
  time, yet with full memory a \emph{faster} controller steers \emph{less} (false controller,
  nosolution: 0.20 at $\lambda_c = 0.5$, 0.05 at $\lambda_c = 8$; confirmed). Common root: every
  fact is true and the whole pool favours the truth, P(A0, A1, A2) = (0.75, 0, 0.25); here the
  more different facts get out and stay, the closer beliefs move to that point (an explanation
  from a correlation, not yet tested directly).
\item \textbf{Sensing goes from unbiased to biased.} The controller now overestimates its
  support, from about $+0.014$ at slow rates to $+0.15$ at $\lambda_c = 8$. Likely cause: the
  posts it reads come from recently active agents, often just after reading its own facts.
\item \textbf{Timing effects the rounds hid.} Fast controllers overshoot and relax (symmetric,
  false controller at $\lambda_c = 8$: 0.26 at $t \approx 4.5$, 0.14 at $t = 30$). The effect
  persists ten time units after the controller stops (change at most 0.016).
\item \textbf{The gap between truth and falsehood widens.} On symmetric the false controller
  loses 0.09 while the truth controller is unchanged; on nosolution both lose and the truth
  controller keeps its lead.
\item \textbf{What carries over, and is therefore more trustworthy}: help with the truth
  replaces the agents' own proofs; the truth controller is stronger than the false one; the
  vote gate saves messages at little or no cost in control; the proof stop changes nothing
  of size $1/24$ (one agent's worth of belief).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Caution.} $\lambda_c$ stands in for $b$ but is not the same: $b$ facts arrive
together each night with no total cap; $\lambda_c$ facts arrive one at a time under a budget of
30 messages. Point 1 is the cleanest comparison; point 2 is by analogy. One world, 24 agents,
probability-matching votes, 1{,}000 episodes per setting. Details:
\url{analysis/task003_llm_free/simulation_2/analysis/2026-10-09_report.md}.
