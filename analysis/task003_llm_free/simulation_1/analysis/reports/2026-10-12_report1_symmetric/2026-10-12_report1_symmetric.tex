\paragraph{How the simulator works.} The world, \texttt{task\_003}, has three candidate
allocations; A0 is the correct one. Each of 24 agents is given one true fact about it and reasons as
an exact Bayesian: its \emph{belief} is the exact probability of A0, A1 and A2 given the facts it
currently remembers. An episode has 30 rounds, each a day and a night. During the day the agents act
one at a time in a new random order: an agent reads $q$ posts drawn at random from a shared
\emph{board} (never its own), adds their facts to its memory, recomputes its belief, \emph{votes}
(\emph{argmax}: the most probable allocation; \emph{probability matching}: a vote drawn from its
belief) and posts one remembered fact that supports its vote, or nothing if none does. At night a
\emph{controller} with a target, A0 (truth) or A2 (false), reads $q_c$ agent posts; unless at least
75\% of them come from agents voting for its target, or what it read already proves its target, it
posts the $b$ facts from its own pool that best support its target. The board is then cleared,
keeping only the controller's new posts. At each dawn every remembered fact is forgotten with
probability $1-\rho$ and comes back if read again. Every episode is run three times with the same
random draws: without a controller, with the truth controller and with the false controller.

\paragraph{Summary.} 24 agents together hold a complete proof that allocation A0 is
correct, but no single agent does. A controller steers toward A0 (truth) or A2
(false). (i)~\textbf{Whether the false controller wins depends on memory}: when agents
forget, it overturns the proof; with full memory its success is temporary, because its
own true facts end up completing proofs of A0. (ii)~\textbf{The controller senses the
swarm accurately}, so its failures are not sensing failures. (iii)~\textbf{Helping the
truth is not the same as helping agents assemble their own proof}: extra truth posts
raise the share of agents holding a proof, but lower the share holding one from their
own evidence.

The two pools share no fact, contain no decisive fact, cannot prove their target, and
are matched in strength pair by pair. The agents start, in expectation, with 9 / 7 / 8
votes for A0 / A1 / A2. Agents are exact Bayesian reasoners: an agent's belief is the
exact posterior over the three allocations given the facts it remembers. Three agent
rules are compared: \emph{argmax} voting with posts that support the vote (the
\emph{base} rule), \emph{probability matching} (vote drawn from the posterior), and
argmax with \emph{uniform} posts (a random remembered fact, whatever the vote).

\paragraph{Measuring control.} Every episode is run three times with the same random
draws: silent, truth controller, false controller. The \emph{paired gain} of a
controller in the share of agents voting for allocation $a$ on day $t$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
G_t^{(a)} = \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\Bigl[x_{a,t}^{\text{ctrl}}(e) - x_{a,t}^{\text{silent}}(e)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
with $E = 1000$ episodes per cell and 95\% intervals from resampling episodes.

The phase diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:phase}) separates the three factors that matter:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Memory $\rho$ decides} whether the false controller can win at all (top
  row against bottom row).
\item \textbf{Reading more defends the proof} under supporting posts: at $\rho = 0.75$
  the gain falls from about 0.9 at $q = 3$ to about 0.3 at $q = 12$. Agents who hold
  pieces of the proof keep posting them, so readers keep meeting them. Under uniform
  posts this defence disappears (Table~\ref{tab:gain}: 0.67--0.72 at every $q$).
\item \textbf{A larger posting budget helps the false controller} ($b = 1 \to 9$:
  0.52 $\to$ 0.79 at $\rho = 0.75$, averaged over $q$): each pool is entirely on its
  target's side, so more posts mean more evidence for A2. The reading budget $q_c$
  matters little.
\end{itemize}

\label{fig:proof}

\paragraph{Why memory decides.} Figure~\ref{fig:proof} shows the mechanism. With
forgetting, a proof must be reassembled continually; while the false controller fills
the board with A2 evidence, the agents' proof decays (own-evidence proofs fall to
zero) and the A0 vote follows. With full memory the opposite happens: agents
accumulate facts, including the controller's A2 facts. \textbf{Those facts are true},
so together with the agents' evidence they complete proofs of A0: on day 30, 95.6\% of
agents hold a proof of A0, but only 42\% from their own evidence. The rest are proofs
the false controller helped build. Every fact a truthful controller posts eventually
narrows the possible worlds toward the real one, and with full memory nothing is lost.

\section{The controller senses the swarm accurately}

Each night the controller estimates the share of agents backing its target from the
$n \le q_c$ agent posts it reads:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vhat}
\hat v = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{1}\{\text{author of post } i \text{ voted for the target}\},
\end{equation}
and stays silent when $\hat v \ge 0.75$. Each agent posts at most once a day, so the
day's board is nearly a census of votes and $\hat v$ is an unbiased sample of it.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_sensing.pdf}
\caption{Left: error of $\hat v$ against the true share of the 24 agents. Right: gate
mistakes. Mean over arms, $q$, $b$, $\rho$; task003-symmetric.}
\label{fig:sensing}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{No bias}: $\hat v$ minus the true share averages within $\pm 0.0005$.
\item \textbf{The error shrinks with $q_c$} as random sampling predicts, and vanishes at
  $q_c = 24$, where the controller reads every post (Figure~\ref{fig:sensing}, left).
\item \textbf{Gate mistakes are rare} (right panel): under 2\% of nights at $q_c \ge 12$.
  They grow at $q_c = 3$, where the gate needs all 3 posts to agree.
\item \textbf{Abstention does not distort it}: agents almost never abstain (0.1\% of
  agent-days, probability matching only).
\end{itemize}
So in this setup, \textbf{a controller fails because of what it posts, not what it
sees.}

\section{How efficiently does the controller steer?}\label{sec:eff}

A controller can change the swarm in two ways: in the direction it is asked to, or in ways that
happen whatever its target. We measure how readable the command is in the swarm, how far each
controller gets toward its target, how much of its change is directed, what each fact it posts buys,
and how unsure the agents become. Throughout, the target is chosen by a fair coin, and all
quantities are taken at one time $t$, across the 1{,}000 episodes of each arm (not over whole
trajectories). Setting: probability matching, $q = 6$.

\subsection{Measures}

\end{tikzpicture}
\diagramcaption{Three distributions of the swarm's state at time $t$, one per arm, each over the
episodes of that arm. The controller's total change $C$ splits exactly into a part that depends on
its target ($I$, from the mixture $Q$ to each arm) and a part that does not ($R$, from silence to
$Q$). Efficiency $\eta = I/C$ is the directed share.}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{Distributions.} For each arm $a$ (silent $0$, target A0 $+$, target A2 $-$) and a
time $t$, each episode $e$ contributes a probability vector $y_a(e)$ over the values $x$ of the
state $X$: at \emph{one-agent} level its vote shares (the vote of a randomly chosen agent), at
\emph{swarm} level a mark on the bin of $s = m_{\text{A0}} - m_{\text{A2}}$ (difference of mean
beliefs, three equal bins on $[-1,1]$). Then $\hat P_a = \frac1n\sum_e y_a(e)$. Any pair of controllers
can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
\hat P_\pm = \hat P_0 + c \pm \Delta,\qquad c = \tfrac12(\hat P_+ + \hat P_-) - \hat P_0,\quad
\Delta = \tfrac12(\hat P_+ - \hat P_-),
\end{equation}
a \emph{common} part $c$ (what control does on average, whatever the target) and a
\emph{directional} part $\Delta$.

\noindent\textbf{Readability and the pair efficiency.} With $\hat Q = \hat P_0 + c$ and
$\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q) = \sum_x p(x)\log_2[p(x)/q(x)]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta}
I = \tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(\hat P_+\Vert\hat Q) + \tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(\hat P_-\Vert\hat Q),\quad
C = \tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(\hat P_+\Vert\hat P_0) + \tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(\hat P_-\Vert\hat P_0) = I + R,\quad
R = \mathrm{KL}(\hat Q\Vert\hat P_0),\quad \eta = I/C .
\end{equation}
$I$ is the mutual information between the target and the state: how readable the command is
(at most 1 bit). $I$ depends only on $\Delta$, $R$ only on $c$; the reference $\hat P_0$ is the silent
arm. For small changes, $\mathrm{KL}(p+\epsilon\Vert p) \approx \sum_x \epsilon(x)^2/(2\ln 2\,p(x))$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etasmall}
\eta \approx \frac{1}{1 + \Vert c\Vert^2/\Vert\Delta\Vert^2},\qquad \Vert v\Vert^2 = \sum_x \frac{v(x)^2}{\hat P_0(x)} .
\end{equation}
So $\eta = 1$ exactly when the two controllers' changes are mirror images ($c = 0$), whatever their
size, and $\eta$ falls only through the \emph{ratio} of common to directional change: when both push
the same way (a common push), or one moves the swarm much more than the other (a lopsided pair:
along one direction with sizes $a$ and $b$, $\Vert c\Vert/\Vert\Delta\Vert = |a-b|/(a+b)$). It is a
property of the pair, not of either controller, and it stays constant whenever both effects grow
together. At swarm level $C$ is often infinite (the controlled swarm reaches states silence never
visits); there we use $\eta_{JS} = I/(I + \mathrm{JS}(\hat Q,\hat P_0))$ with the bounded
Jensen--Shannon divergence.

\noindent\textbf{Each controller against its own target} $z$ (one agent, votes):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:progress}
S_z = \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Bern}(\hat P_z(z))\,\Vert\,\mathrm{Bern}(\hat P_0(z))\big),\qquad
\pi_z = \frac{S_z}{\log_2[1/\hat P_0(z)]},\qquad
\varphi_z = \frac{S_z}{\mathrm{KL}(\hat P_z\Vert\hat P_0)},\qquad
\text{price} = \frac{S_z}{\text{facts posted}} .
\end{equation}
$S_z$ is the least information any intervention must inject to raise the target's share from its
silent value to the observed one (raise the target, scale the other answers in proportion).
\textbf{Progress} $\pi_z$ is the share of the way from silence to unanimity on the target, in bits;
\textbf{focus} $\varphi_z$ the share of the controller's actual change that was needed for that gain;
both are negative when the controller moved its target \emph{down}. The \textbf{price} is the
information injected per fact the controller posted. Finally, the agents' own uncertainty:
$\Delta\bar H$ (mean entropy of their beliefs) and $\Delta$JSD (disagreement, the entropy of the mean
belief minus $\bar H$), controlled minus silent, same episode.
